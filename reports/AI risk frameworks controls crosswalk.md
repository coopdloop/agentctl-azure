# Fifty machine-checkable controls unify agent AI governance

As of October 2026, no single framework governs tool-using LLM agents, but a dozen overlap heavily, and they converge on the same six demands. Every agent needs **its own identity**, **least-privilege tools**, **human approval for high-impact actions**, **independent and complete logging**, **layered prompt-injection defence**, and **a named human owner with a working kill switch**. The governance backbones (NIST AI RMF 1.0, NIST AI 600-1, ISO/IEC 42001, Microsoft's RAI Standard v2) all predate agents and contain no tool or action controls. The agent-specific detail comes from newer threat taxonomies: the OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications 2026 (ASI01–ASI10), OWASP Agentic Threats & Mitigations v1.1 (T1–T17), the beta OWASP MCP Top 10 and MITRE ATLAS v2026.09. It also comes from voluntary government guidance, chiefly the Five Eyes "Careful Adoption of Agentic AI Services" (May 2026). The EU AI Act is the only binding law in the set. The **Digital Omnibus, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, is now adopted** and moves Annex III high-risk duties to **2 Dec 2027**. For most enterprise agents today, that leaves Arts. 4, 5, 25 and 50 as the obligations that already apply. On Azure, 30 of the 50 controls in a unified catalog can be checked fully automatically (and 15 more partially) from ARM properties, RAI-policy (guardrail) fields, Azure Policy compliance, Defender for Cloud recommendations and alerts, Entra Agent ID objects in Graph, Foundry agent definitions and App Insights traces. The remainder (impact assessments, training, red-team sign-off, incident plans, disclosures) needs expiring manual attestations bound to agents through metadata. The catalog below defines 50 controls with stable IDs. Each control is mapped across the frameworks, scoped to environment, Foundry account or agent, and tied to a specific evidence signal. Preview and unverified signals are flagged throughout.

## Twelve frameworks split into spines, threat lists and law

The frameworks are not competitors. They play three roles. **Governance spines** define management processes. **Threat taxonomies** name the attacks and the technical mitigations. **Law and government guidance** set obligations and baselines. A control catalog needs all three: the spines for accountability, the taxonomies for engineering, and the law for deadlines.

### Governance spines carry accountability but no agent mechanics

**NIST AI RMF 1.0** has not been replaced. NIST says only that it "is being revised as part of the White House AI Action Plan", and has published no draft or date ([NIST AI RMF](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework)). None of its subcategories is agent-specific. Agent controls therefore derive from a handful of general ones: MAP 3.3 (targeted application scope), MAP 3.5 (human oversight processes), GOVERN 1.6 (inventory), GOVERN 1.7 and MANAGE 2.4 (mechanisms to "supersede, disengage, or deactivate" systems, effectively the kill switch), MEASURE 2.4 (production monitoring) and MEASURE 2.7 (security and resilience) ([NIST AI 100-1](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.100-1.pdf)). **NIST AI 600-1** (26 Jul 2024) defines 12 GAI risks, from CBRN to Value Chain and Component Integration. It treats both direct and indirect prompt injection under Information Security. Agents appear only once, as an item in MS-2.7-001's threat list. The single explicit prompt-injection action is MS-2.7-007, which calls for red-teaming. Deactivation is covered by GV-1.7-001 and MG-2.4-004 ([NIST AI 600-1](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf)). NIST's agent-specific work is still pre-normative. CAISI launched its AI Agent Standards Initiative on 17 Feb 2026 ([CAISI](https://www.nist.gov/caisi/ai-agent-standards-initiative)). The NCCoE concept paper "Accelerating the Adoption of Software and AI Agent Identity and Authorization" is an initial public draft ([CSRC](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/other/2026/02/05/accelerating-the-adoption-of-software-and-ai-agent/ipd)). COSAiS lists single-agent and multi-agent SP 800-53 overlays only as "proposed use cases" ([CSRC COSAiS](https://csrc.nist.gov/projects/cosais)). A catalog should pin "AI RMF 1.0 (Jan 2023)" and plan to re-map once the revision or COSAiS agent overlays land.

**ISO/IEC 42001:2023** is the certifiable counterpart: an AI management system following the Harmonized Structure, plus a normative Annex A of 38 controls across A.2–A.10 ([ISO](https://www.iso.org/standard/81230.html)). The most useful Annex A controls for agents are A.4.4 (tooling resources), A.6.2.5 (deployment), A.6.2.6 (operation and monitoring), A.6.2.8 (recording of event logs), A.8.4 (communication of incidents), A.9.2–A.9.4 (responsible and intended use) and A.10.2–A.10.3 (responsibility allocation and suppliers). These titles come from a secondary listing because the standard is paywalled ([RiskProfs](https://riskprofs.com/iso-42001-annex-a-controls-list/)). Authentication and least privilege are deliberately left to ISO/IEC 27001, which an AIMS typically integrates. **ISO/IEC 23894:2023** adapts ISO 31000 to AI. Its Annex B risk sources include "level of automation" and "complexity of environment", which give the ISO-side justification for agent risk entries ([ANSI preview](https://webstore.ansi.org/preview-pages/ISO/preview_ISO+IEC+23894-2023.pdf); [OECD.AI](https://oecd.ai/en/dashboards/policy-initiatives/information-technology-%E2%80%94-artificial-intelligence-%E2%80%94-guidance-on-risk-management-isoiec-23894)). ISO/IEC 42005:2025 now supplies impact-assessment guidance for A.5 ([ISO 42005](https://www.iso.org/standard/44545.html)).

**Microsoft's Responsible AI Standard v2** (June 2022) organises 17 goals under six principles. It supplies process controls rather than technical ones: A1 impact assessment (updated at least annually), A5 human oversight and control (A5.1–A5.7), T3 disclosure of AI interaction, RS2 failures and remediations, and RS3 ongoing monitoring ([Microsoft RAI Standard v2](https://blogs.microsoft.com/wp-content/uploads/prod/sites/5/2022/06/Microsoft-Responsible-AI-Standard-v2-General-Requirements-3.pdf)). **CSA's AI Controls Matrix v1.1** (July 2026) is the largest catalog: 247 controls in 18 domains. One domain, Model Security (MDS-01 to MDS-13), is AI-native, and v1.1 adds AIUC-1 to its existing mappings to ISO 42001, the AI RMF and the EU AI Act. AICM has no agent domain; CSA covers agents in its MAESTRO threat model and its Agentic AI Red Teaming Guide ([CSA Labs AICM v1.1 note](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-aicm-v1-1-update-20260730-csa-styled/); [CSA intro](https://cloudsecurityalliance.org/articles/introducing-the-csa-ai-controls-matrix-a-comprehensive-framework-for-trustworthy-ai)). In AICM's shared-responsibility model, an enterprise building on Foundry is mainly the **Application Provider and AI Customer**. Microsoft is the Cloud Service Provider and Orchestrated Service Provider.

### Threat taxonomies supply the agent-specific engineering

**OWASP's 2025 LLM Top 10** is still current. Its agent-critical lines are LLM06's "complete mediation", meaning "implement authorization in downstream systems rather than relying on an LLM to decide if an action is allowed", and LLM07's warning that privilege separation and authorization checks "must not be delegated to the LLM" ([LLM06](https://github.com/OWASP/www-project-top-10-for-large-language-model-applications/blob/main/2_0_vulns/LLM06_ExcessiveAgency.md); [LLM07](https://github.com/OWASP/www-project-top-10-for-large-language-model-applications/blob/main/2_0_vulns/LLM07_SystemPromptLeakage.md)). The **OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications for 2026** (9 Dec 2025) has ten entries:

- ASI01 Agent Goal Hijack
- ASI02 Tool Misuse and Exploitation
- ASI03 Identity and Privilege Abuse
- ASI04 Agentic Supply Chain Vulnerabilities
- ASI05 Unexpected Code Execution
- ASI06 Memory & Context Poisoning
- ASI07 Insecure Inter-Agent Communication
- ASI08 Cascading Failures
- ASI09 Human-Agent Trust Exploitation
- ASI10 Rogue Agents

Its Appendix A maps every entry to LLM IDs and to T-IDs ([OWASP ASI 2026](https://genai.owasp.org/resource/owasp-top-10-for-agentic-applications-for-2026/); [ASI PDF](https://genai.owasp.org/download/52117/?tmstv=1765059207)). **Agentic AI – Threats and Mitigations v1.1** adds T16 Insecure Inter-Agent Protocol Abuse (MCP, A2A) and T17 Supply Chain Compromise. Its T9 explicitly names theft of "a formal, persistent agent identity (e.g., Microsoft Entra Agent ID)" as a threat ([T&M PDF](https://genai.owasp.org/download/45674/?tmstv=1739819891)). The **OWASP MCP Top 10** is still beta ([OWASP MCP Top 10](https://owasp.org/www-project-mcp-top-10/)). The MCP Security Cheat Sheet supplies the most concrete tool-integrity practices, including hash-pinning tool definitions and re-prompting for consent "when tool definitions change" ([OWASP cheat sheet](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/MCP_Security_Cheat_Sheet.html)). Use ASI IDs for reporting, and use the T-IDs and MCP IDs for control design.

**MITRE ATLAS now ships monthly**. The latest release is v2026.09 (15 Sep 2026), and several technique names have changed:

- AML.T0053 is now "AI Agent Tool Invocation" (formerly LLM Plugin Compromise).
- AML.T0056 is now "Extract LLM System Prompt".
- AML.TA0001 is now "AI Attack Adaptation".

Newer agent techniques include T0080 AI Agent Context Poisoning (Memory/Thread), T0086 Exfiltration via AI Agent Tool Invocation, T0101 Data Destruction via AI Agent Tool Invocation, T0110 AI Agent Tool Poisoning, T0109 AI Supply Chain Rug Pull and T0034.002 Agentic Resource Consumption. The agent mitigations are M0026–M0033 (agent permission configuration, HITL, restricting tool invocation on untrusted data, memory hardening, segmentation, I/O validation) and M0036–M0038 ([ATLAS data 2026.09](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mitre-atlas/atlas-data/main/dist/v6/ATLAS-2026.09.yaml); [manifest](https://github.com/mitre-atlas/atlas-data/blob/main/dist/manifest.yaml)). In the parsed data, **T0110 and T0109 have no mapped mitigations**, so the crosswalk borrows OWASP's signed, hash-pinned tool definitions to cover them. **Google SAIF**'s risk map lists 15 risks. Three of its controls are agent-specific: Agent Permissions, Agent User Control and Agent Observability, the last marked "New". They map onto "Rogue Actions" and "Sensitive Data Disclosure" ([saif.google risks](https://saif.google/secure-ai-framework/risks); [controls](https://saif.google/secure-ai-framework/controls)). The same three ideas appear as principles in Google's secure-agents paper: well-defined human controllers, limited powers, and observable actions ([Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/an-introduction-to-googles-approach-for-secure-ai-agents/)).

### Law and government guidance set the deadlines and the baseline

The **EU AI Act Digital Omnibus is law**. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 has applied since 27 July 2026. It moves Annex III high-risk obligations to **2 Dec 2027** and Annex I obligations to **2 Aug 2028**. It softens Art. 4 literacy to "measures to support" literacy, allows an Art. 27 FRIA to cross-reference a GDPR DPIA, adds two Art. 5 prohibitions from 2 Dec 2026, and centralises supervision of GPAI-based systems from the same provider group in the AI Office ([AI Act Explorer – Omnibus](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/ai-act-explorer/digital-omnibus/); [Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/07/EU-AI-Act-Update-Digital-Omnibus-Finalizes-8-Compliance-Changes)). An enterprise building agents on Azure-hosted models is usually a downstream provider and/or deployer of an AI *system*. OpenAI and Microsoft carry the GPAI-model duties in Arts. 53 and 55, and both signed the GPAI Code of Practice ([EC GPAI Code](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/contents-code-gpai)).

The duties that apply today are Art. 4 (literacy), Art. 5 (prohibitions), Art. 50(1) (telling people they are interacting with AI) and Art. 50(2) (machine-readable marking, with a grace period to 2 Dec 2026 for legacy systems). Art. 25 also matters: repurposing a general agent for an Annex III use such as HR screening or credit makes the enterprise a high-risk **provider** ([Art. 25](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/25/); [Art. 50](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/)). From December 2027, Annex III agents face the full high-risk stack:

- Art. 12 automatic logging, with logs kept at least six months under Arts. 19 and 26(6)
- Art. 14(4)(e), a "stop" button that halts the system in a safe state
- Art. 15(5), resilience against data poisoning, adversarial examples and confidentiality attacks
- Art. 73 incident reporting within 15, 10 or 2 days depending on severity

([Art. 12](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/12/); [Art. 14](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/); [Art. 15](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/15/); [Art. 73](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/73/)). Building these capabilities into the platform now costs little compared with retrofitting them, because Art. 25 can reclassify any agent overnight.

**The Five Eyes guide "Careful Adoption of Agentic AI Services"** (1 May 2026; CISA, NSA, ASD's ACSC, CCCS, NCSC-NZ, NCSC-UK) is the most agent-specific government baseline. It groups agent risk into privilege, design/configuration, behavioural, structural and accountability risks. It recommends:

- a verifiable identity per agent with temporary credentials
- least privilege, with autonomy expanded gradually
- human checkpoints before an agent escalates to higher-risk actions
- *independent* monitoring that cross-validates agent self-reports against system logs
- layered prompt-injection defences

([CISA](https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools/resources/careful-adoption-agentic-ai-services); [Covington](https://www.covingtonblogs.com/2026/05/28/cisa-releases-guidance-on-the-careful-adoption-of-agentic-ai-services/); [Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2026/06/multi-agency-guidance-on-securing-agentic-ai-systems)). The primary PDF was not read for this report, so its exact section names are unverified. In the UK, the **DSIT AI Cyber Security Code of Practice** (31 Jan 2025) has 13 principles. Two of its provisions map straight onto agent controls: 2.6 limits AI systems' permissions over other systems, and 8.3 requires an audit log of changes to system prompts and model configuration ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-cyber-security-code-of-practice/code-of-practice-for-the-cyber-security-of-ai)). The Code became **ETSI TS 104 223 V1.1.1** (April 2025), with 72 trackable provisions ([ETSI](https://www.etsi.org/newsroom/press-releases/2521-etsi-technical-specification-sets-international-benchmark-for-securing-artificial-intelligence/)). In the US, **OMB M-25-21** binds only federal agencies. Its minimum practices for high-impact AI (pre-deployment testing, impact assessment with signed risk acceptance, ongoing monitoring, operator training, human oversight with fail-safe, appeals, feedback) are a useful template for agent approval gates ([OMB M-25-21](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/M-25-21-Accelerating-Federal-Use-of-AI-through-Innovation-Governance-and-Public-Trust.pdf)). Federal policy otherwise leans deregulatory: EO 14365 pushes to pre-empt state AI laws, but those laws remain in force ([Ropes & Gray](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2026/03/examining-the-landscape-and-limitations-of-the-federal-push-to-override-state-ai-regulation)). Singapore's IMDA Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI (22 Jan 2026) states that organisations remain legally accountable for what their agents do ([Bird & Bird](https://cm.twobirds.com/en/insights/2026/singapore/singapore-introduces-new-model-ai-governance-framework-for-agentic-ai)).

## Azure exposes evidence for most controls, with preview gaps

Microsoft's own normative layer is the **MCSB v2 Artificial Intelligence Security domain**:

- AI-1 approved models
- AI-2 multi-layered content filtering
- AI-3 safety meta-prompts
- AI-4 least privilege for agent functions, which names Entra Agent ID, deny-by-default capability manifests and short-lived tokens
- AI-5 human-in-the-loop
- AI-6 monitoring and detection
- AI-7 continuous red teaming

MCSB v2 is itself in **public preview**, and Microsoft warns that it "might not reflect the latest developments in … agentic AI controls" ([MCSB v2 AI](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/security/benchmark/azure/mcsb-v2-artificial-intelligence-security)).

Seven evidence surfaces matter, and each has known caveats:

1. **The Foundry account ARM resource** (`Microsoft.CognitiveServices/accounts`, kind `AIServices`, latest GA API 2026-07-01) exposes the infrastructure controls as properties: `disableLocalAuth`, `publicNetworkAccess`, `networkAcls.defaultAction`, `restrictOutboundNetworkAccess` plus `allowedFqdnList`, `encryption.keySource`, `networkInjections[scenario='agent']`, `userOwnedStorage` and `capabilitySettings` ([ARM accounts](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/templates/microsoft.cognitiveservices/accounts)).
2. **Guardrails are ARM `raiPolicies`**. The 2026 schema adds agent intervention-point sources (`PreRun`, `PreToolCall`, `PostToolCall`, `PostRun`), an `action` enum that includes `HITL`, `safetyProviders`, and a hosted-agent `egressPolicy` that defaults to Deny ([ARM raiPolicies](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/templates/microsoft.cognitiveservices/accounts/raipolicies)). Two caveats are critical. **Tool-call and tool-response guardrails are preview, and apply only to a fixed list of tools that excludes MCP.** An **agent guardrail "fully overrides" the model's guardrail**, so an agent guardrail without tool controls leaves tool calls unscanned ([Guardrails overview](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/guardrails/guardrails-overview); [intervention points](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/foundry/guardrails/intervention-points)).
3. **Azure Policy** has built-ins for key access, network, Private Link, CMK, managed identity, diagnostics, approved models (`aafe3651…`) and model eligibility (`8791d062…`), plus preview content-filter policies for deployments and **agents** (`595f98a5…`, `9224c1cc…`) ([Policy reference](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/policy-reference)).
4. **Defender for Cloud** offers GA AI threat protection with `AI.Azure_*` alerts, including `AI.Azure_AnomalousToolInvocation` and `AI.Azure_ASCIISmuggling` ([alerts](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/defender-for-cloud/alerts-ai-workloads)). Its preview AI-SPM recommendations evaluate agents directly, for example "Foundry AI Agent with Indirect prompt Injection risk should require human in the loop control for MCP tool actions" and "Foundry Agent's MCP tool should be configured with allowed tools list" ([AI recommendations](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/defender-for-cloud/recommendations-reference-ai)). Defender ships an EU AI Act regulatory standard but **no NIST AI RMF or ISO/IEC 42001 standard** ([regulatory standards](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/defender-for-cloud/concept-regulatory-compliance-standards)).
5. **Entra Agent ID is GA** and modelled as `agentIdentity` service principals. These carry `sponsors`, `owners`, inherited permission grants, a `lifecycle` relationship and an `attest` action ("Attest to the continued need for this agent identity") ([What's new](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/agent-id/whats-new-agent-id); [agentIdentity](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/graph/api/resources/agentidentity?view=graph-rest-beta)).
6. **The Foundry agents API** (`{project}/agents?api-version=v1`) returns `definition.instructions`, `definition.tools[]` (with MCP `require_approval`, `allowed_tools`, `server_url`), `metadata` (≤16 pairs), `state`, and `instance_identity`/`blueprint` with `principal_id`/`client_id` ([Foundry agents REST](https://ai.azure.com/api-reference/agents/); [MCP how-to](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/agents/how-to/tools/model-context-protocol)).
7. **App Insights traces** carry OpenTelemetry GenAI spans (`invoke_agent`, `execute_tool`, `chat`), with message-content capture **opt-in** ([semconv-genai](https://github.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai/blob/main/docs/gen-ai/gen-ai-agent-spans.md); [Trace agents](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry-classic/how-to/develop/trace-agents-sdk)).

One gap matters for the design. **No documented field on the Foundry agent object shows which guardrail (raiPolicy) an agent uses.** A per-agent guardrail check must therefore rely on the preview Azure Policy agent content-filter definitions or the Defender recommendation verdicts, or fall back to the deployment's `raiPolicyName` when the agent inherits. Evidence for the remaining controls (impact assessment, literacy training, incident plans, Art. 50 notices) cannot be read from Azure, which is why Defender greys such controls out as not automatically assessable. These belong in attestations that carry an owner, an evidence link, a date and an expiry, keyed into agent `metadata` (for example `ia_id`, `redteam_run`). A new agent version that ships without a fresh attestation then shows up automatically as drift.

## A unified catalog of 50 controls across 11 domains

The catalog design follows from the evidence surfaces above. Control IDs are stable two-letter domain prefixes plus a sequence number:

| Prefix | Domain |
|---|---|
| GV | Governance |
| ID | Identity & access |
| NW | Network |
| DP | Data protection |
| GR | Guardrails |
| TL | Tools & actions |
| MO | Monitoring |
| SC | Supply chain |
| TE | Testing & evaluation |
| IR | Incident & oversight |
| TR | Transparency |

Scope says where the check runs: **Env** (tenant or subscription), **Acct** (Foundry account or project) or **Agent** (agent name and version). Evidence type is **AUTO** (fully automated signal), **PARTIAL** (an automated signal corroborates a manual attestation) or **MANUAL** (attestation only, with the evidence to capture). Status flags: **[GA]**, **[Preview]**, and **[Unverified]** for a signal or path that was not confirmed against a primary source.

The mappings use these conventions:

- LLM = OWASP LLM Top 10 2025.
- ASI = OWASP Agentic Top 10 2026, with T-IDs and MCP IDs added where they sharpen the mapping.
- RMF = NIST AI RMF 1.0 subcategory; IDs such as GV-1.6-001 are NIST AI 600-1 actions.
- ISO = ISO/IEC 42001 Annex A, titles per a secondary listing.
- AIA = EU AI Act article; **†** marks high-risk-only duties, which apply to Annex III systems from 2 Dec 2027.
- ATLAS = v2026.09 technique (T) and mitigation (M) IDs, with the AML. prefix omitted.
- MCSB = MCSB v2 AI-x, preview; "—" means the control falls outside the AI domain.

### Governance (GV)

| ID | Control and description | Mappings | Scope | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|

## Unverified items shape the build order

Three implementation choices follow from the gaps. First, the guardrail checks (GR-02 to GR-04) should be **read defensively**. The ARM `contentFilters[].name` strings for indirect attack, PII and task adherence are unconfirmed, as is the agent-to-guardrail reference, so an evaluator should normalise names case-insensitively, keep unknown names, and treat the preview Azure Policy agent definitions and the Defender recommendations as the authoritative verdicts until Microsoft documents the field. Second, **MCP tools are a guardrail blind spot**. Tool-call and tool-response scanning does not cover them ([Guardrails overview](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/guardrails/guardrails-overview)), and ATLAS maps no mitigation to tool poisoning or rug pulls. TL-02, TL-03 and TL-04 are therefore compensating controls, not optional extras, for any agent that uses MCP. Third, several evidence paths below rest on standard Azure patterns rather than confirmed documentation and need validation in a test tenant before they gate anything:

## Conclusion

The difference between frameworks is mostly vocabulary. Where they really disagree is on *whose* control it is. Microsoft, as Orchestrated Service Provider, now ships the primitives: per-agent Entra identities, guardrails at the tool boundary, MCP approval flags and Defender checks that evaluate agent definitions. Using them remains the customer's job, and several default to off or to preview. Legacy agents run on the shared project identity. Content capture, egress restriction and `require_approval` are all opt-in. An agent-level guardrail silently drops the model's guardrail. A control plane that continuously checks these 50 controls is therefore less about compliance reporting than about catching configuration drift that no single framework would name.

The most consequential design decision is to make **agent `metadata` and Entra sponsors the join keys between automated evidence and human attestations**. Once intake, impact-assessment, threat-model, eval and red-team IDs travel with each agent version, a new version automatically shows which human judgements are stale. Platform-side reconciliation (MO-03) turns the Five Eyes demand for independent monitoring into a query rather than a promise. EU high-risk duties do not apply until December 2027, which gives time to build these capabilities before any deadline forces them. The Art. 25 reclassification risk means they should exist before the first agent is repurposed for an Annex III use, not after.
